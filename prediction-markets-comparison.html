How a question becomes a market
Polymarket vs Kalshi

Both platforms decide what you can bet on — but they answer to very different gatekeepers. One is editor-controlled with crypto-native resolution; the other is a federally-registered exchange that has to clear contracts past the CFTC.

Polymarket — editor-controlled

The Polymarket markets team decides what gets listed. Users can suggest, never deploy. Settlement is decentralized through UMA's optimistic oracle.

Kalshi — regulated exchange

A CFTC-registered Designated Contract Market. Every contract type either gets formal approval (45–90 days) or self-certifies and goes live in 10 days unless the regulator blocks it.

Polymarket

Suggestion → review → on-chain deployment
  1. User submits a suggestion
    Via Twitter @polymarket, Discord (#polymarket-ideas, #submit-a-polymarket), or email. Strong proposals include a clear yes/no question, a specific publicly-verifiable resolution source, a defined end date in UTC, and evidence of demand.
  2. Internal review by the markets team
    Dedup against existing markets, scrutinize the resolution source (named URL/feed, not "as reported by major news"), and tighten edge-case wording. Most rejections are for ambiguous criteria.
  3. Approval, revision, or rejection
    Approved proposals get scheduled for deployment. Rejections come with a brief reason; resubmission is free.
  4. Smart-contract deployment on Polygon
    The market goes live as an on-chain contract. Polymarket or a designated LP seeds initial USDC liquidity so traders can enter immediately.
  5. Resolution via UMA's optimistic oracle Decentralized
    A proposer asserts the outcome; anyone can dispute within a window; disputes are settled by UMA token-holder vote. Listing is centralized; settlement is not.

Kalshi

DCM filings under CFTC oversight
  1. Kalshi designs the contract
    Defines underlying event, source agencies (e.g. BLS, Fed, NYT, AP, Reuters), expiration, settlement procedure, and price bands. Must comply with the Commodity Exchange Act.
  2. Choose the regulatory path
    Two routes under CFTC Part 40 — Kalshi has used both, sometimes after one fails:
    Request for Approval 45–90d
    45d (extendable to 90)

    Conservative route for genuinely novel products. Full submission package; CFTC can deny.

    Self-Certification 10d
    10d auto-effective

    Kalshi attests CEA compliance. Goes live unless the CFTC affirmatively blocks within the window.

  3. CFTC review (or silence)
    Approval path: explicit CFTC blessing required. Self-cert path: the burden of stopping the listing flips to the regulator — silence equals go.
  4. Listing under an umbrella contract
    Once an umbrella contract is certified (e.g. POLITICALECONOMICEVENTS with pre-named source agencies), Kalshi can spin up specific instances — particular Fed decisions, jobs prints, elections — at its own discretion without re-filing.
  5. Resolution via Kalshi rulebook
    Settlement follows the named source agency. Rule 7.1 Market Outcome Review covers ambiguous expirations, all under federal oversight.

The pivot that unlocked Kalshi

2023 — Request for Approval

"Congressional Control Contracts" denied

Kalshi submitted political-control contracts via the formal approval route. The CFTC denied them, calling them "gaming." Kalshi sued; District Court ruled for Kalshi in Kalshi v. CFTC (Sept 2024).

2024 — Self-Certification

Sports contracts go live nationwide

For sports event contracts, Kalshi switched to self-certification. The CFTC didn't act inside the 10-day window — contracts went live. That's how Kalshi offers sports markets in all 50 states while DraftKings and FanDuel are stuck at ~40.

Why the asymmetry matters

Speed of origination

Polymarket can spin up a market in hours once review clears. Kalshi pays a 10-day self-cert clock or a 45–90-day approval clock — unless an umbrella is already in place.

Regulatory reach

Kalshi's CFTC status preempts state law on event contracts. Polymarket is permissionless on-chain but had to negotiate its US comeback separately.

Where markets fail

Polymarket markets fail on resolution ambiguity — the UMA dispute layer. Kalshi markets fail on regulatory categorization — is this gaming, is the source authoritative, is the umbrella stretched too far?

Trust model

Polymarket: token-holder vote on outcomes. Kalshi: rulebook + federal oversight. Different answers to "who do you trust to settle this?"
